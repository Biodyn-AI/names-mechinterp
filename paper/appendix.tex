\section*{S1 Study scope and data construction}

This study uses fictional registries that explicitly list objects, their properties and their names. It does not include the earlier country-fact experiments or four-world pilots. The generator fixed 32 development worlds and 64 worlds reserved for confirmation. Only the development worlds were run through a model. Files named REGISTRATION record the inputs and code fixed before each local run, linked by file hashes. They are not public preregistration. The later six-world diagnosis was planned after the validation failed and does not provide independent confirmation.


Each world contains four permanent object IDs, four different values for each of color, shape and home, and four statements assigning aliases to objects. Two aliases name the same object; another names a distractor, and a fourth supplies context. A nameplate is a label, not a permanent ID. The eight selected alias words were apple, bat, anchor, date, cat, basket, fig and dog. We chose them using only how both models' tokenizers divide the prompts into tokens, without looking at model answers. Individual words appear in more than one world, but the complete ordered assignments of values to objects do not repeat.


Each world supplies 96 prompts: two orders of the equivalent names, four Focus conditions, and twelve questions per context. The twelve questions comprise nine factual questions about three aliases, two identity questions, and one registry-status question. Focus is a prompt highlight: it is absent, names either equivalent alias, or names an unrelated alias. Swapping the equivalent names between their binding lines does not change which object either name refers to. Across worlds, we balance the target record's position, alias-line positions, object IDs, the roles assigned to names, and two wording formats. This balance does not cover every possible combination of factors. Both models receive the same worlds and 3,072 prompts each; this is not a sample of 64 independent worlds.


\section*{S2 Fixed model versions, execution and answer grading}

Each model uses its fixed tokenizer and chat template. We use eager attention, process one prompt at a time, use float32 arithmetic, disable TensorFloat-32, and use one CPU thread. At each step, we retain the model's original, or native, output scores for its entire token vocabulary at one output position. Greedy generation chooses the highest-scoring next token. We grade the entire decoded answer after normalizing case, whitespace and final punctuation. We do not remove explanations to turn a response into a correct answer; invalid or extra text remains an error. The intervention runs allow eight new tokens and record whether generation stops on an end-of-sequence token. None of the final diagnostic answers was cut short by the token limit.

\section*{S3 Search sequence and the six validation cases}

The first exploratory search replaced internal outputs at the end-of-alias marker, before the prompt stated the requested field. It tested 35 layer boundaries and then attention heads in two selected layers, using eight development cases. A separate search at the answer position used the same eight cases. In that search, the donor computation already included the requested field. We compared attention to the correct and wrong records across all 36 layers and 32 heads, then selected two candidates. This selection used the correct-answer labels. The later operation transferred the attention shares for all four records without taking answer labels as inputs. With the best candidate, layer 28, head 0 with counting starting at zero, the first token became correct in five of eight cases. This was a promising preliminary result, not a measurement of complete-answer repair.


We then fixed that head and tested the six remaining eligible development worlds. They were separate from the eight search cases, but we had already examined their behavioral answers. A case qualified when the model answered all three fields and the identifier question correctly using the donor alias. With the equivalent recipient alias, it also had to give a completed answer from another listed record for at least one factual question. The recipient questions included eight wrong answers and ten initially correct answers, which we checked for damage. The final diagnosis split the intervention into parts and tested them on these same six worlds, without changing the component or cases.

\section*{S5 Nine fixed interventions and what they change}

We summarize attention in three ways. Record mass is the sum of attention probabilities on the tokens in one printed object record. The record profile is each record's share of the total attention on all records. The within-record pattern is each token's share of its own record's attention. The four profile/pattern conditions in the table keep the recipient's total attention on records unchanged. The donor and recipient value vectors at record positions must be identical byte for byte. These are the internal vectors that attention combines to form a head's output. We change that output before the attention output projection, the step that mixes head outputs. Calculations use float64 coefficients and float32 contractions, or weighted vector sums. For each field, we hold the change fixed at the original answer-query position throughout answer generation. We do not move it to newly generated positions. For the random control, matching the norm means matching the length of the whole-head change vector.

\section*{S6 Pre-set success criteria, failed tests and statistical limits}

The original validation required at least four of eight errors to be repaired across at least three worlds. It allowed at most one initially correct, protected answer to become wrong. It also required at least two more repairs than a fixed average profile from discovery. Reverse and cyclic interventions each had to produce at least nine of eighteen specified record answers across at least three worlds. Cyclic success also required at least two target IDs. These steering targets were scored separately from factual correctness. The primary profile repaired one error, in world 029. Reverse and cyclic tests each reached one specified target. No intervention passed its full set of success criteria, and no model runs on the untouched confirmation worlds followed.


The final nine-condition diagnosis generated 162 answers. The seven new conditions, comprising six targeted changes and the random control, each repaired zero of eight errors. Replaying the old profile reproduced its one repair. Every condition preserved the ten initially correct, protected answers. This rejects the simple repair tested here, not every possible way a model could connect names to objects. The saved scores for the first answer token show that the changes did affect the model. We measured the score of the single token for the prescribed correct answer, called the canonical token, minus the score of the fixed first token of the original wrong response. This margin improved by 6.00 on average for the old profile, 4.05 for the same-field profile, and 4.21 for whole-head transfer. These describe score changes, not complete-answer accuracy or probability sums over accepted answer variants.


For the behavioral intervals, we resample whole worlds 2,000 times, keeping each world's repeated questions and conditions together. These describe variation in the development data; they are not confirmation tests and do not treat questions as independent samples. Additional wrong-record intervals use the retrospective seed 20261115. Predictive comparisons keep entire worlds out of fitting when testing on them. Intervals calculated with the saved predictions held fixed do not include uncertainty from refitting or model selection.


\section*{S7 Numerical checks and links to the original records}

We set a maximum difference of 0.00002 for reconstructing the unmodified head output and for drift at the fixed query position during generation. The observed query drift was zero. Record profiles require total attention mass of at least 0.0001 and attention on each record of at least 0.00000001. Checks of full-head reconstruction, recombination of record and residual parts, and the random change's norm all passed. Thirty-six unmodified forward passes replayed exactly. Eighteen self-control and eighteen old-profile complete-answer trajectories also matched exactly, including their generation steps. The CPU audit recomputed component changes from saved tensors and checked recorded native scores; it did not rerun model inference. A separate grader read the literal prompts and agreed with the answer grades. The final run used 360 native forward passes, meaning actual model calculations. We did not change a failed success criterion, the component or the intervention strength to obtain success.
